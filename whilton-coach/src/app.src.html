Whilton Mill line guide

Where to brake, how hard, and which kerbs to use, corner by corner.

Start here

Four novices, one phone, a pair of earphones. Do these in order. First, the track: the line, coloured by how hard you brake, with every corner named. Tap it for the full map and the kerb verdicts.

1. Before the day: learn the lap

Ten minutes a day, each of you, earphones in. By race day the corner names, what your feet do at each one and the rhythm of the lap are in your head.

    2. Test it at home: the walk test

    Prove the phone can follow you before you trust it in a kart. Press Start walk test, allow location, and walk round the garden or the block. The dot should follow you within a few metres and it says how good the signal is. Then press Test the sound with the earphones in.

    2b. Practice loop: walk the whole lap in a field

    The full coach, without the circuit. It lays a shrunk copy of Whilton Mill on the ground where you stand, and you walk or jog round it following the dot on the map: every chirp, word and tone at the corners, the lap time at the line, and one thing to change after each lap. Jog where it says flat, walk where it says brake, and it will coach you exactly as it will in the kart. You need an open space about a hundred metres across and a clear view of the sky.

      Debrief and the team table

      Coaching compares each corner with your own best through it, and with what the guide expects there: flat, lift or brake. It looks at where you started slowing, your slowest speed and how soon you were accelerating again. From the second lap it picks one focus corner, the one costing most against your best, and keeps at it until it improves, then tells you what you found. Next lap that corner's brake word carries the cue, "Hard brake, brake later". The table shows every lap corner by corner with the model's time alongside. A loaded file needs latitude, longitude and time columns: time as seconds, milliseconds, a clock or a date stamp. Speed is read as m/s unless the column name or a units row says km/h, mph or knots.

      What you hear, and the settings
      • Words: Hard brake, Firm brake, Dab, Lift, Flat. They match levels 5 to 1 on the map.
      • Tone pitch: high for full braking, lower for firm and light braking, a soft hum for a lift or for coasting to the apex after braking. Silence means full throttle.
      • The tone starts a fraction early to allow for your reaction time. If you find yourself braking late, raise the tone lead.
      • With a phone giving one fix a second, the start of a tone is good to about 10 metres and its end to rather less, because a braking zone only lasts a second or so. At 10 to 25 fixes a second the receiver stops being the limit. What is left is the map itself, traced from a drawing and locked to your trace with a fit error of a few metres (shown under the Start GPS button), and the brake zones, which are estimates until you calibrate them from your own laps.
      • The word lead counts from when the word starts, not when it ends, and phones take a moment to start speaking. Run the demo with your earpiece in and raise the word lead until the word has finished before the tone starts.
      • A rising chirp comes a second before each brake word, so you are ready and the word only confirms the point. Switch it off above if you find it noise.
      • From the second lap the coach predicts where you will be from your own last lap through that stretch, not just from your current speed, which steadies the calls into the corners on a phone's one fix a second.
      • Learn my brake timing: the word lead grows by how long this phone takes to start speaking, measured; and if your braking keeps landing later than the plan at a corner over three laps, the word and chirp there move earlier by the difference, up to 20 metres. With a phone's one fix a second the brake point is too coarse to learn from, so that part only acts with a 10 Hz or faster receiver. The debrief lists any corner that has been moved. The tones are not moved.
      • At one fix a second the coach stays quiet unless it has a reason: a rule (you lifted where it is flat, braked early), or an apex slower than your best on two laps running. Corner times alone are noise at that rate and are never spoken.
      • With the driver level set to Model, the tones and words follow the physics model's fastest lap for the hire kart on its own line. That is the limit for a perfect driver, so its brake points will feel late and its lifts short. Use it to hear where time is, not as a first-day target.
      • In the wet, Talk me round uses general wet sentences (off the dry line, no kerbs, gentle) rather than the corner guides. The one written Whilton-specific wet instruction, UK Karting\'s late turn-in and snap to full lock at The Boot, is on that corner\'s card.
      How it works at the circuit

      A tone sounds for exactly as long as your foot should be off the throttle. Its pitch tells you how hard: a low hum is a lift, a high tone is full braking. A chirp and then a word a moment earlier tell you what is coming, and two quick rising notes mean back to full throttle.

      1. On the Map tab set the chicane on or off, the driver level and dry or wet before you start. The lock finds International or National from your trace, but it cannot tell the chicane apart. The lap runs clockwise; the arrow at the start line shows the direction.
      2. Check GPS here works anywhere: ten seconds of fixes, then how many came, how accurate they were and how far you are from the circuit. Press Start GPS, wait for it to say GPS ready, zip the phone away and drive. After one full lap the app fits the track map to your GPS trace, says Map locked, and remembers the lock for next time. If the circuit is running the other layout today it re-locks itself once a quarter of your fixes fall off the map, or press Forget map lock before you start.
      3. From then on you get the tones and the words, and at the next quiet moment after the line the lap time with the gap to your best and one coaching point. If the phone loses its fix it says GPS lost and goes quiet, then GPS back when it returns. It cannot see flags: a yellow flag overrides anything it says.
      4. In the pits, read the debrief below. The venue times every session and prints your laps: compare them with the lap times here, because at one fix a second the app can be a few tenths out on the line. Its monthly rental leaderboard shows what quick hire drivers do on each layout.

      Hearing it inside a helmet

      • Kart coaches already talk to drivers through in-ear race earpieces worn under the helmet, so the listening side is proven. The foam-tipped or custom-moulded kind sold with kart radio systems blocks engine noise and sits flat under the helmet padding. Ordinary earbuds tend to get dragged out as the helmet goes on.
      • Wire them to the phone if you can. Bluetooth adds roughly a fifth of a second. If you use it, add 0.2 to both lead times above. Most phones now have no headphone socket, so you will need the phone maker's USB-C or Lightning to 3.5 mm adapter, and race earpieces usually end in a 3.5 mm mono plug or a radio connector, so check it fits or get a lead that does. Route the cable inside the suit and out at the collar so nothing can catch on the wheel.
      • Carry the phone the way coaching radios are carried: zipped inside the suit or strapped to the rib protector, never loose. A phone against your body sees less sky, so check the accuracy readout before trusting it. In hire kit you will have neither a closed pocket nor a rib protector, so ask the desk whether a phone in an inside pocket or an armband is allowed. If it is not, keep this app for your own kit.
      • Set the volume with your helmet on and an engine running nearby. You should still be able to hear karts around you.

      Before you take it on track

      • Ask the circuit first. Hire circuits generally ban phones and loose items on track and may not allow earpieces; Whilton's own written rule was not found, so ask at the desk before you sign on. Club100 rules say pit-to-kart radio is not permitted, and assume any other race series is the same until you have read its regulations. For a hire driver the realistic place for this is an ordinary arrive-and-drive session with the venue's permission, or the venue's Arrive and Drive PLUS coaching session; Whilton's Owner Practice days are for drivers with their own karts.
      • Never hold the phone or look at it while driving. The lap timer ignores the start line below about 18 mph, so a pit-lane pass does not count as a lap.
      • Press Test the sound once before every session, with the phone set up exactly as you will run it, and check you hear both the word and the tone.
      • If location is blocked when you press Start GPS, open this page in Safari or Chrome instead of inside the Claude app, or put the downloaded copy on any HTTPS web host.
      • Keep the screen on. The app asks the browser to keep it awake, which works in Chrome for Android and in Safari on iOS 16.4 or later, but browsers stop GPS once the phone locks. As a backstop set the screen timeout as long as your phone allows, and on an iPhone turn off Low Power Mode, which forces a 30 second lock. Lock the phone to this page so pocket touches cannot stop it: Guided Access on an iPhone, app pinning on Android.
      • On an iPhone, turn off silent mode (the side switch, or the Action button or Control Centre on newer models) or the tones, and probably the words, will not play.
